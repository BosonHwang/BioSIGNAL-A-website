Short Summary
Clinical artificial intelligence for biosignal analysis is proliferating quickly, yet the benchmarks evaluating them do not fully capture real clinical needs and use cases. This paper introduces the BioSIGNAL-AI consortium, motivated by clinically grounded benchmarking. The consortium invites clinicians, AI researchers, biomedical engineers, ethicists, and regulatory experts to participate.









Clinical AI models are being evaluated in ways disconnected from practice, a phenomenon which is well documented and widely ignored across medical imaging, risk prediction, and clinical language models 1. Such observations generalise across various domains, but their mechanism does not. In imaging and text, the model and the clinician examine the same object. For biosignals, the disconnect is not only in what task is chosen; it is already present in the data before any task is defined. What has changed is the weight that the disconnect now carries1.

Biosignal data, such as electrocardiography (ECG), electroencephalography (EEG), photoplethysmography (PPG), inertial measurement unit (IMU) data, electromyography (EMG), and respiratory signals, becomes clinical information only through a chain of acquisition and processing choices – sensor placement, device, filter settings, segmentation, and quality gating – that vary across sites, devices, and signal modalities. Such choices are seldom recorded along with the data. The clinician is usually only consulted at the end of this dynamic processing chain, but the AI model is scored on the predetermined pipeline from raw waveforms to diagnoses. Crucially, the evaluation of such models has long converged on a small set of accessible public corpora, including PTB-XL, TUEG, Sleep-EDF, and the PhysioNet challenge sets, scored on aggregate discrimination 2–4. These corpora are valuable public goods. The challenge is the role they have come to play: they define not only what a model sees, but what question it is asked. Rhythm classification dominates ECG modelling because rhythm labels exist; five-stage scoring dominates EEG modelling because staged annotation is what polysomnography produces. Each is a legitimate task, but none was selected because a clinician identified it as the binding constraint on care.

Biosignal foundation models raise the consequence of this mismatch. A task-specific model makes a narrow claim, and its benchmark validates only that claim. A foundation model makes a claim of generality: its representations transfer across a wide range of downstream clinical uses, and the evidence offered is the breadth of benchmark coverage, often advertised as a count of downstream tasks 3. However, that count is a function of how many labelled public corpora contain, not of how many clinical decisions a model would inform. The available corpora also share an acquisition regime. Models presented as general across ECG are developed and evaluated almost entirely on clinical-grade twelve-lead recordings, while the generality claim extends to single-lead patches and wrist-worn sensors 2. The benchmark suite thereby ceases to be a test of the generality claim and becomes a model’s operational definition.

In practice, the field has no clinical consensus on what a biosignal AI model should be general throughout, so the available corpora supply one by default. As such, there is a distinction worth naming: (a) a clinically grounded task begins from a documented care gap and is scored against the decision it informs; or (b) a benchmark-driven task begins from the statistically significant labels an accessible dataset carries. The field works almost entirely on the second kind. The biosignal AI community has settled to this state through no failure of effort or expertise, but through incentives that make it rational at every step. Public corpora are easily accessible and pre-labelled. Reusing them buys comparability with prior work, and comparability is what makes a result credible. What travels with those corpora is more than a task list. Every dataset embeds inclusion criteria, such as signal quality gating, artefact rejection, and wear-time thresholds, chosen to make modelling tractable and validated against signal-level references rather than against how a clinician would act on the recording. Such criteria are rarely neutral with respect to the underlying clinical question, and a model engineered from available cleaned data might not fully capture the complexities of recordings during an actual clinical procedure, making real-world adoption almost impossible. The alternative is expensive and unowned. It requires identifying unmet needs with clinicians, curating representative multicentre data, defining endpoints by consensus, and standardising analysis pipelines. None of this rewards the publication cycle, and the full cost falls on whoever initiates it, whilst the field takes the benefit 4,5. A clinically grounded evaluation standard is thus a public good that everyone needs yet no one is incentivised to build.

However, the underprovision of that public good is not cost-free. For a task-specific model, the cost was at least bounded: a benchmark that misdescribed rhythm classification misdescribed one model performing one task. A foundation model propagates the same error across every downstream application its generality claim licenses, including applications its developers never examined, and its adopters cannot audit. What was a local measurement error becomes an inherited property of the infrastructure. Presacan et al.’s paper illustrates the disparity between technical measurement and actual clinical use. Deep learning reconstructions of 12-lead ECGs from a single lead scored well on aggregate error and passed a downstream rhythm-classification check, yet were judged unfit for clinical use as the spatial information the missing leads carry was never recovered and the model hallucinated, resulting in a regression-to-the-mean effect 6. No existing benchmark highlighted this because what was lost lay in localised morphology, which no accessible label indexes and no aggregate metric can identify. This reflects that whilst a model can satisfy every benchmark the corpora supports, it can provide no clinical utility. Presacan et al.’s model failure was detectable due to the claim being slim enough to compare against a clinical read. In contrast, a foundation model makes the same class of error almost impossible to identify; meaning the real danger lies where models are used in cases where their developers might not have intended or stress-tested, on recordings in ways the training corpora never covered, or benchmarks that entirely missed this issue altogether. 

The consequences are distributed. Practicing clinicians are seldom told what a model was validated on: what population was used, what device was used to record previous signals, or what recording condition was used, whether because developers see no need or because consultation is logistically taxing. Accountability is another issue that has not been settled: where a decision informed by such a model proves wrong, responsibility is contested between the clinician who acted and the developer whose evidence of fitness was a benchmark score that never described the patient in question. Least visible is the equity cost, since benchmarks built on non-representative corpora render underperformance in under-represented groups not merely unaddressed but unmeasurable 7. The field pays as well, in talent and computation directed toward models that may never reach a clinic, and in the discounting that follows repeated failure at translation.

We propose the BioSIGNAL-AI consortium, a multidisciplinary initiative to establish consensus on two questions: (a) which clinical problems should be prioritised as benchmarking tasks; and (b) how should those tasks be translated into clinically grounded benchmarks? Rather than beginning with a predetermined suite of available datasets and leaderboards, the BioSIGNAL-AI consortium aims to provide a shared path from clinical need to benchmark design. The initiative will develop task prioritisation principles to identify problems that address meaningful care gaps and inform defined clinical decisions. Simultaneously, benchmarking principles will be constructed, covering intended use, target population, endpoints, metrics, comparators, and validation 8. These principles will combine a shared core with modality-specific extensions. The shared core will address issues across biosignal modalities, including the relationship between development data and the intended-use population and clinical setting, clinically grounded distribution shifts, endpoint validity, subgroup performance, calibration, and external validation. Modality-specific panels will translate this core according to the physiological and measurement properties of different biosignals. For example, EEG benchmarks may need to account for noise, artefacts, montage and reference dependence, whereas ECG benchmarks may need to consider relationships between leads, electrode placement, lead availability, and preservation of diagnostically important morphology. The resulting consensus will inform a prioritised task portfolio and a benchmark specification template. A worked exemplar will be developed using the candidate principles, revised in light of the Delphi process, and subsequently advanced into a pilot benchmark.

For the research community, the consortium alters the economics of the biosignal AI community. The expensive work of identifying unmet needs with clinicians, curating representative multicentre data, and defining endpoints by consensus is undertaken as shared infrastructure, so costs no longer fall on whoever attempts it while the field takes the benefit. Reuse of existing corpora was rational because it bought comparability; a shared reference point supplies that without inheriting the task definitions attached to the corpora. Results become cumulative, and researchers without clinical collaborators acquire a target worth optimising toward. Representativeness built in rather than added afterwards improves robustness generally, and early regulator engagement aligns benchmark criteria with approval criteria. Through an implementation science lens, the more consequential change is upstream of any dataset. Where clinically grounded parameters are agreed in advance, variables a clinician regards as non-negotiable are included by design rather than surviving a filter that selects on statistical contribution alone. 

For clinicians and patients, the consequence follows by construction. Where models are judged against clinically grounded tasks and clinical-utility endpoints, those the field rewards are those closer to being usable, and its stated aspirations (eg. atrial fibrillation detection at scale, seizure prediction in resource-limited settings, early warning of deterioration from continuous waveforms) become reachable because evaluation now selects for them. Specifications that clearly state intended use, target population, and endpoints also give clinicians a basis for knowing what a model was tested on before deciding whether it applies to the patient in front of them. Models can be built to be more accountable and closer to implementation, which is the precondition for trust that the medical community requires.
To develop these principles, we will first launch a multispecialty and multidisciplinary Delphi study. Deciding what to benchmark and against what metrics requires judgment on clinical priorities, patient outcomes, measurement validity, and acceptable risk; none of which benchmark developers can supply alone. Candidate principles will be derived from the literature, existing frameworks, and initial stakeholder consultation. Through successive rounds, participants will anonymously assess their importance, clarity, feasibility, and applicability, review structured feedback, and propose revisions 9. All participants will evaluate the shared core, while those with relevant expertise will also contribute to modality-specific extensions. Participants will also nominate candidate clinical tasks, which will be assessed using the task prioritisation principles. The protocol, consensus thresholds, and planned subgroup analyses will be preregistered. We will report both agreement and unresolved disagreements, along with participant composition, attrition, and protocol deviations. We invite clinicians, patients and patient representatives, AI researchers, biomedical engineers, benchmark and dataset developers, ethicists, regulators, and other relevant stakeholders to join the BioSIGNAL-AI consortium through https://bosonhwang.github.io/BioSIGNAL-A-website/.
